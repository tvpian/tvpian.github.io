How can robots learn from demonstrations and corrections while retaining earlier skills?

A correction gives the robot a new example to learn from. The next question is whether it can handle that case while keeping its earlier skills.

Learning from People

Robots learning from people need a way to turn individual interventions into reusable experience. This ongoing work investigates a correction loop connecting demonstrations, policy failure, human takeover and retraining. People and policies use the same bounded arm commands, so their rollouts can be compared step by step and reused for training. The implemented workflow records corrections for review before they are added to the demonstrations used to train the next policy. Policy evaluation is currently performed manually. Whether the corrections improve later policies without eroding earlier skills remains an open question.

Related simulation studies compare teacher-student training with learned auxiliary heads to examine what policies retain when privileged training inputs are removed. A portable egocentric interface is also in development to extend demonstration capture beyond teleoperation. Together, these efforts investigate how different forms of human guidance can support continuing learning through a shared recording and review workflow.

Simulation studies
What policies retain from training

Simulation tests compared teacher-student training with learned auxiliary heads to see what policies retain when privileged inputs are removed. Attention maps help diagnose behavior, but they do not by themselves establish a causal explanation.

What has a policy learned, and how does that shape its behavior?

A probe asks what information is present inside the policy. An intervention tests whether changing that information changes what the robot does.

Policy Explainability and Representation

Information can be available within a policy without determining what it does. This ongoing work examines that distinction through representation probes and activation patching in simulation, alongside instruction-conditioned trials on a bimanual robot. In the policies tested in simulation, associations between words and objects were present in the representations but were not used by the action heads. Physical trials separated object choice from pick completion. In those trials, higher task success did not necessarily mean the policy was better at choosing the object named in the instruction.

These findings motivate testing the information a policy encodes separately from the information that guides its actions. Current work develops evaluation beyond success rate, including partial progress, recovery and repeatability. The broader research question is whether understanding learned representations can help improve learning itself. The findings reported here remain scoped to the tested policies and comparisons.

How can predicting what happens next help a robot learn how to act?

What might happen next, and what action could make it happen? Predictive models and inverse dynamics approach action learning from those two directions.

Predictive Models, Inverse Dynamics, and Transfer

Video describes how a scene changes, but useful predictions do not by themselves establish that useful actions have been learned. This work investigates predictive models and inverse dynamics as complementary routes from visual experience to action. A published video-action model was adapted to teleoperation data and evaluated offline. In simulation, actions inferred from video were compared with recorded actions and commands sent to the robot. Future-feature prediction, instruction grounding and failure signals were assessed separately from task success.

The evidence so far is limited to offline and simulation studies. Physical validation is pending. Active experiments use a digital twin to study how changes in sensing, timing, contact and appearance affect transfer between simulation and hardware. The central question is which parts of a predictive or action representation remain useful when a policy must operate in a different setting.

How can robots understand what people are doing and what they intend?

A click, an image, a phrase, or a gesture can express an intention. The challenge is turning that incomplete evidence into something a robot can act on.

Understanding People

Understanding people requires combining incomplete observations with the different ways a person can express an intention. This line of work examines that problem through multi-view pose recognition and multimodal instruction for embodied systems. PoseFusion, a UMD graduate course project, tested camera-view fusion for action recognition under occlusion. In the reported tests, combining views raised F1 from 0.76 to 0.82 compared with a single view. In the co-developed Track Anything Rapter system, work on the high-level controller connected target specification by click, box, image or text to aerial tracking. A trajectory evaluation compared the physical flight trials against motion capture.

An exploratory policy on the NatSGD dataset provided by Snehesh Shrestha (UMD) combined demonstration, speech and gesture. Task success and the benefit of gesture remain unvalidated. Together, these studies motivate the continuing question of how complementary human cues can support both understanding and action when the evidence is incomplete.

Platform details and evaluation tools

Comparing robot-learning methods requires a workflow that keeps demonstrations, policy behavior and deployment conditions connected. The research platform was designed and implemented to link teleoperation and synchronized recording to dataset conversion, policy training, evaluation and physical deployment. The physical robot and digital twin share operator, data review and policy interfaces, with separate hardware and physics backends. This supports moving experiments between simulation and hardware without rebuilding the surrounding workflow.

Policy evaluation is currently performed manually. The platform provides the experimental setting for the research threads above and for developing evaluation measures that examine behavior beyond task success. The shared infrastructure is implemented; the research questions studied through it remain open at the stages described in each thread.

U-ASTROD · GameSec 2024

Multimodal anomaly detection

For the ASTROD research program, I architected the fusion pipeline that encoded LiDAR, odometry, and network traffic independently before combining their learned representations in a shared anomaly detector.

The published system paired modality-specific spatiotemporal encoders with feature-wise reconstruction scoring. It achieved up to 98% accuracy with vector-based reconstruction loss versus 72% with scalar-based loss, and 97% with half the training data.

Chapter 02 · 2021–23Formative research exploration

Multimodal human instruction

Learning robot actions from demonstrations, speech, and gesture

Using the NatSGD dataset provided by Snehesh Shrestha (UMD), I implemented a kitchen-object detector, a language-conditioned recurrent policy, and two experimental gesture pathways. The notebooks establish working model and training prototypes, while task success and the benefit of gesture remain unvalidated.

Contribution boundary: I did the notebook implementation; the NatSGD dataset and paper belong to Shrestha and his co-authors. The model builds on Stepputtis et al.'s language-conditioned imitation-learning method.
