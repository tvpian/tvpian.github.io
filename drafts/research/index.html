How can robots learn from demonstrations and corrections while retaining earlier skills?

A person's intervention can be more than a way to finish a task. It can become a demonstration the robot learns from.

Study notes

When a policy fails, a person can take control of the arm. The workflow records the correction so it can be reviewed and added to the demonstrations used to train the next policy.

People and policies use the same arm commands, so their actions can be compared step by step. Policy evaluation is currently performed manually. Whether the corrections improve later policies without eroding earlier skills remains an open question.

In development
Egocentric demonstrations

A portable interface is designed to capture human manipulation demonstrations using the same recording and review tools as teleoperation.

Simulation studies
What policies retain from training

Simulation tests compared teacher-student training with learned auxiliary heads to see what policies retain when privileged inputs are removed. Attention maps help diagnose behavior, but they do not by themselves establish a causal explanation.

Current workSimulation and physical robot studies

What has a policy learned, and how does that shape its behavior?

A policy can represent which object a word refers to without using that knowledge when it acts. Understanding the representation is only part of the question.

Study notes

In simulation, representation probes showed that the tested policies encoded links between words and objects. Activation patching showed that their action heads did not use those links.

Bimanual robot trials measured object choice separately from pick completion. In those trials, higher task success did not necessarily mean the policy was better at choosing the object named in the instruction.

In development
Policy evaluation beyond success rate

The goal is to compare policies on partial progress, failure modes, recovery and repeatability as well as task success.

How can predicting what happens next help a robot learn how to act?

What might happen next, and what action could make it happen? Predictive models and inverse dynamics approach action learning from those two directions.

Study notes

Offline tests evaluated a video-action model adapted to teleoperation data. Simulation tests compared actions inferred from video with recorded actions and commands sent to the robot. They assessed future-feature prediction, instruction grounding, and failure signals separately from task success.

Active experiments
Transfer between simulation and hardware

Experiments use a digital twin to study how changes in sensing, timing, contact and appearance affect transfer between simulation and hardware.

U-ASTROD · GameSec 2024

Multimodal anomaly detection

For the ASTROD research program, I architected the fusion pipeline that encoded LiDAR, odometry, and network traffic independently before combining their learned representations in a shared anomaly detector.

The published system paired modality-specific spatiotemporal encoders with feature-wise reconstruction scoring. It achieved up to 98% accuracy with vector-based reconstruction loss versus 72% with scalar-based loss, and 97% with half the training data.

Hybrid IK for plant-safe planning

A plant is not a rigid obstacle with a clean bounding box. This system first estimates roots, leaf spread, and bed geometry, then couples learned plant geometry with a hybrid inverse-kinematics controller so the manipulator can reach weeds while reducing unintended contact.

Chapter 02 · 2021–23Formative research exploration

Multimodal human instruction

Learning robot actions from demonstrations, speech, and gesture

Using the NatSGD dataset provided by Snehesh Shrestha (UMD), I implemented a kitchen-object detector, a language-conditioned recurrent policy, and two experimental gesture pathways. The notebooks establish working model and training prototypes, while task success and the benefit of gesture remain unvalidated.

Contribution boundary: I did the notebook implementation; the NatSGD dataset and paper belong to Shrestha and his co-authors. The model builds on Stepputtis et al.'s language-conditioned imitation-learning method.

Research direction

Robots that can understand people and learn from them.

My long-term aim is to develop algorithms that allow robots to interpret human intent in ways that support action and learning.

My work approaches this problem through policy understanding, representation learning, world models, embodied experience, human interaction, continual learning, and rigorous evaluation.

I am particularly interested in methods that remain interpretable and testable when they move from offline benchmarks to physical systems.

I hold an M.Eng. in Robotics from the University of Maryland and bring experience across academic research, industrial R&D, and real-world robotics deployment.
